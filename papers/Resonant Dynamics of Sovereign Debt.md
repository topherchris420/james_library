ABSTRACT
CBO's February 2026 baseline projects federal debt held by the public rising from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 120
percent by 2036, while net interest costs increase from 3.2 percent of GDP in 2025 to 4.6 percent by 2036. Those projections
do not imply an imminent sovereign crisis. Still, they reflect a persistent deterioration in the interest-growth differential and
a rising probability that the fiscal trajectory becomes a material financial-stability concern — not only domestically, but as a
determinant of global dollar-funding conditions. This paper examines three structural drivers that compound that trajectory
beyond what conventional budget analysis captures: reserve currency costs, security expenditure asymmetries, and
deindustrialization. It then develops a productive-capacity framework as a complement to fiscal consolidation, arguing that
durable debt stabilization requires both credible primary-balance adjustment and policies that sustain trend growth strong
enough to keep the interest-growth differential manageable. The productive-indispensability concept introduced in Section 4
is operationalized through a four-criterion quantitative scoring methodology and further adjusted for geopolitical fragility —
the recognition that supply-chain indispensability concentrated in contested jurisdictions is conditional rather than structural.
The Treasury-market transmission channel in Section 5 is calibrated against the 2022–2023 regional banking episode
through a duration-based capital-impact framework and extended to the cross-border channels through which a domestic
fiscal trajectory becomes an international one: global collateral demand for Treasury securities, the foreign-exchange swap
basis, and the structural reliance of foreign banking organizations on U.S. wholesale dollar funding. Drawing on Federal
Reserve speeches by Bernanke and Yellen, the 2025 Financial Stability Report, BIS commentary on sovereign-debt risk and
cross-currency funding, and IMF work on interest-growth differentials, the paper maps the supervisory implications of
elevated sovereign debt into specific transmission channels: valuation risk in securities portfolios, wholesale funding
pressure — domestic and cross-border — policy-space compression, and Treasury-market functioning. The central
conclusion is that fiscal consolidation and productive-capacity investment are complements rather than substitutes, that
treating them as such is a prudential rather than ideological position, and that U.S. debt stabilization functions as much as a
foundational input to global financial stability as it does a domestic fiscal exercise.

1. Introduction
On December 29, 2006, the United Kingdom made its final payment on a $4.34 billion loan contracted in 1945, when
Britain emerged from the Second World War carrying sovereign debt exceeding 249 percent of GDP. That sixty-one-year
repayment arc is a useful reference point not because the United States faces an analogous near-term constraint, but because
it illustrates a mechanism that standard fiscal accounting tends to underweight: the debt-to-GDP ratio is as sensitive to the
growth rate of the denominator as to the growth rate of the numerator. Britain's nineteenth-century reduction of public debt
from approximately 260 percent of GDP in 1815 to roughly 30 percent by 1900 resulted substantially from the productivity
gains of industrialization rather than from austerity (Office for Budget Responsibility, 2023). The same arithmetic, working
in reverse, explains how obligations incurred at 249 percent in 1945 required six decades to discharge.
The United States faces a different situation: larger productive reserves, a more entrenched reserve currency position, and no
prospect of formal default under foreseeable conditions. But the trajectory of the fiscal accounts warrants supervisory
attention on its own terms. CBO's February 2026 baseline projects debt held by the public rising from 101 percent of GDP in
2026 to 120 percent by 2036, while net interest costs increase from 3.2 percent of GDP in 2025 to 4.6 percent by 2036
(Congressional Budget Office, 2026; Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, 2026). CRFB's summary of that baseline
notes that debt would surpass the post-World War II record by FY 2030. The Federal Reserve has repeatedly identified the

long-run fiscal path as a legitimate macro-financial concern. Bernanke emphasized that debt-to-income ratios cannot rise
without limit and that fiscal adjustments will occur one way or another; Yellen warned that high and rising debt can restrain
capital formation, productivity, and growth (Bernanke, 2010, 2011; Yellen, 2010). More recently, the Federal Reserve's

Treasury yield movement, whatever their proximate cause, serve as reminders that sovereign-financing conditions can
interact with broader market functioning in ways that compress the time available for supervisory response. Because the
dollar intermediates the large majority of global trade invoicing and cross-border bank funding, those same episodes
propagate beyond U.S. borders through channels — global collateral demand, the foreign-exchange swap market, and the
balance sheets of the foreign banking organizations operating in the United States — that this paper treats as integral to,
rather than separate from, the domestic supervisory analysis.
This paper is written from the perspective of financial-institution supervision rather than partisan budget politics. The
analytically relevant question for a Federal Reserve policy analyst is not whether the United States will imminently default.
It is whether the present fiscal trajectory raises the probability of higher term premia, fixed-income valuation losses, tighter
credit conditions, and reduced policy space in future downturns — domestically, and, through the channels developed
below, internationally. Those are prudential questions as much as fiscal ones, and they require attention to the interaction
between sovereign borrowing, Treasury-market functioning, bank balance sheets, and the economy's productive capacity.
The paper advances four analytical contributions. First, it identifies three structural headwinds — reserve currency costs,
security expenditure asymmetries, and deindustrialization — that have compounded the fiscal gap independently of specific
budget decisions and that have no direct counterpart in most advanced-economy fiscal comparisons. Second, it develops a
productive-capacity framework connecting trend growth to debt dynamics through the interest-growth differential, arguing
that frontier innovation, energy infrastructure, and advanced manufacturing belong in any supervisory analysis of fiscal
sustainability — and that the indispensability of the sectors involved must be assessed net of the geopolitical fragility of the
jurisdictions in which they are concentrated, not merely on the basis of domestic engineering and capital-investment
requirements. Third, it maps the supervisory implications of elevated sovereign debt into specific transmission channels
relevant to financial institution oversight. Fourth, it extends those channels across borders, showing how a deteriorating U.S.
fiscal trajectory propagates into global collateral markets, the foreign-exchange swap basis, and the funding decisions of
foreign banking organizations whose U.S. branches are structural net borrowers of overnight wholesale dollars —
converting what could be analyzed as a contained domestic balance-sheet problem into a determinant of dollar-funding
conditions in Europe and Asia. The conclusion is not that the United States is on the verge of default, nor that productive
investment can substitute for fiscal adjustment. It is that the two are complements, that supervisory analysis should treat
them as such, and that the stakes extend beyond U.S. financial institutions to the global financial architecture the dollar
underpins.
2. Fiscal Sustainability and Debt Arithmetic
Fiscal sustainability, for purposes of this paper, means a debt-to-GDP ratio that is stable or declining over time — the
standard formulation used by the IMF, the CBO, and in Federal Reserve speeches by Bernanke (2011). The standard debt
dynamics identity provides the core analytical framework:
(Debt/GDP)_t = [(1 + r_t) / (1 + g_t)] × (Debt/GDP)_{t-1} + PrimaryDeficit_t / GDP_t
Where r is the average nominal interest rate on outstanding government debt and g is nominal GDP growth. When r exceeds
g — when the interest-growth differential is positive — the debt ratio tends to rise even when the primary balance is near
zero. When g exceeds r, debt can stabilize more readily without requiring large primary surpluses. This identity clarifies
why the same fiscal deficit can be benign in one macroeconomic environment and destabilizing in another. Policy can affect

both terms: lowering the primary deficit reduces the second term; raising trend growth improves the first term by narrowing
r–g. IMF work on interest-growth differentials in advanced economies emphasizes that r–g is not a fixed parameter — it is
endogenous to policy, demographics, productivity, and market expectations (IMF, 2018). That endogeneity is central to the
productive-capacity argument developed in Section 4.
That endogeneity also has a cross-border dimension that domestic debt-arithmetic exercises often suppress. The rate r is not
set in a closed economy. It reflects, in part, the price at which a deep pool of non-U.S. official and private holders is willing
to fund the outstanding stock of Treasury debt, and the price at which non-U.S. banks are willing to fund their own dollar
books using that same debt as collateral. Section 3.1 develops global demand for Treasuries as the world's primary collateral
asset as a structural determinant of r in its own right, distinct from, but interacting with, the reserve-currency financing-cost
benefit discussed there.
An important complication comes from Blanchard's 2019 AEA presidential address, which argues that r below g has
historically been the norm rather than the exception in advanced economies, and that when r < g persistently, even large
primary deficits may not be indefinitely unsustainable (Blanchard, 2019). That argument deserves direct engagement rather
than dismissal. If Blanchard's historical characterization is correct, it complicates the urgency framing of this paper's central
argument. Two responses are relevant. First, the current U.S. fiscal trajectory may represent precisely the regime shift away
from the low-r environment Blanchard describes: CBO's 2026 baseline projects net interest costs rising from 3.2 to 4.6
percent of GDP over a decade, a trajectory inconsistent with a persistently r < g regime at the projected debt levels. Second,
Blanchard himself acknowledges that the r < g condition is not guaranteed and that high debt raises the probability of
adverse fiscal dynamics if that condition reverses. The productive-capacity framework developed here is, in part, an
argument about maintaining the conditions — higher trend g, broader tax base, sustained external demand for dollar assets
— that make Blanchard's more benign scenario more likely to persist.
Current public projections point in the wrong direction on both terms of the identity. CBO's baseline shows deficits
averaging 6.1 percent of GDP over the 2026–2036 decade — well above any plausible primary-balance stabilization level
(Congressional Budget Office, 2026). Net interest costs are projected to reach 4.6 percent of GDP by 2036, exceeding the
historical peacetime share of defense spending and representing the fastest-growing component of the federal budget.
Spending reaches 24.4 percent of GDP by 2036 while revenue rises only to 17.8 percent, leaving a structural gap not closed
by either the baseline growth assumptions or the current revenue trajectory.

KEY SUPERVISORY FINDING
The interest-growth differential is the central variable. Under CBO's baseline, r exceeds g persistently through the projection
window, meaning the debt ratio rises even if primary deficits are held roughly constant. Policies that raise trend growth
directly improve r–g and reduce the primary surplus required for stabilization.

3. Three Structural Headwinds
Standard fiscal analysis treats the U.S. deficit primarily as the output of spending and revenue decisions adjustable by
Congress. That framing is accurate as far as it goes, but it misses three structural mechanisms that have compounded the
deficit independently of specific budget choices and that have no direct counterpart in most advanced-economy
comparisons. Supervisory analysis that omits them will systematically underestimate the structural gap between revenues
and obligations.
3.1 Reserve Currency Costs
The dollar's reserve currency status confers genuine financial benefits. McKinsey Global Institute (2009) estimated the net
annual borrowing-cost benefit at $40 to $70 billion — roughly 0.3 to 0.5 percent of GDP — primarily through a 50 to 60
basis point reduction in U.S. Treasury rates. That estimate reflects conditions prevailing before the post-2008 monetary
environment and should be treated as broadly indicative of order of magnitude rather than as a current precise figure; the
convenience yield on U.S. Treasuries is subject to ongoing empirical estimation and varies with global risk conditions. More
recent analysis suggests reserve status increases maximal sustainable debt by approximately 22 percent of GDP
(CEPR/VoxEU, 2024). Those advantages are real and directly relevant to the r–g differential.
They are, however, accompanied by potential offsets that standard fiscal accounting may not fully reflect. Reserve demand
has been associated in the literature with exchange rate levels above those that underlying productive competitiveness alone
might support — McKinsey Global Institute (2009) places the estimated overvaluation at roughly 5 to 10 percent — which
some analysts associate with competitive pressure on tradable goods producers, a dynamic that may have contributed to the
structural shift toward financial activity discussed in Section 3.3, though isolating this channel from other determinants of
sectoral composition is not straightforward. The dollar's share of allocated global foreign exchange reserves has declined
from approximately 71 percent in 1999 to approximately 58 percent by end-2023 per IMF COFER data — a trajectory
documented systematically by Arslanalp, Eichengreen, and Simpson-Bell (2022), who attribute part of the decline to active
portfolio diversification by reserve managers into nontraditional currencies rather than to passive valuation effects alone.
Both the pace of that diversification and its implications for the borrowing-cost premium remain actively debated in the
literature.
This reserve-erosion trend can be stated as an explicit transmission mechanism rather than left as a parallel narrative
observation. The balance-of-payments identity requires that Current Account + Financial Account + Capital
Account ≈ 0 (net of errors and omissions), where the current account decomposes as CA = (Exports − Imports) +
Net Primary Income + Net Secondary Income. A persistent U.S. current account deficit is, by accounting identity,
financed by a financial account surplus — net foreign acquisition of U.S. assets, including Treasuries. As the dollar's reserve
share contracts, the same current-account-deficit-driven financing need must increasingly be met by a narrower pool of
price-insensitive official reserve managers and a wider pool of price-sensitive private investors. Holding the current account
deficit constant, this compositional shift in the financial account requires a higher term premium to clear an unchanged
financing need — directly widening the r–g differential modeled in Section 2.
The borrowing-cost framing above, while standard in the fiscal-sustainability literature, understates the systemic stakes of
reserve erosion for a financial-institution supervisor, because it treats the decline as a price effect on U.S. issuance rather
than as a structural threat to the global stock of safe collateral. Treasury securities are not merely a financing instrument for
the U.S. government; they are the principal high-quality liquid asset against which large parts of the global financial system
— foreign central banks, foreign commercial banks, money market funds, and the FX swap market — fund themselves. As
of December 2025, foreign holders accounted for approximately $9.2 trillion, or 31 percent, of the $30.1 trillion in Treasury
debt held by the public, of which 41.9 percent ($3.9 trillion) was held by foreign official institutions — principally central
banks holding Treasuries as the core of their FX reserve and intervention buffers — and 58.1 percent ($5.4 trillion) by
private foreign investors, including the commercial banks that hold Treasuries as high-quality liquid assets under their home

This dual role — financing instrument and global collateral asset — is the mechanism through which a deteriorating U.S.
fiscal trajectory can generate a global collateral shortage rather than only a domestic financing-cost increase. If term premia
widen in response to fiscal deterioration (the mechanism modeled in Section 5.4), the mark-to-market value of the Treasury
portfolios held by foreign central banks and foreign commercial banks falls through the same duration mechanics as the
domestic bank portfolios analyzed in Table 11. For a foreign central bank, this is a valuation loss on its primary intervention
and liquidity buffer. For a foreign commercial bank, it is a valuation loss on the asset it posts as collateral in its own
domestic repo and derivatives markets and holds to satisfy liquidity coverage requirements. Both effects tighten the supply
of usable collateral at precisely the moment global funding markets are most likely to need it — a mechanism the
cross-currency funding literature documented for 2008, when dealers identified an acute shortage of high-quality Treasury
collateral as a binding constraint on repo and FX swap market function (Hördahl and King, 2008).
The scale of the underlying exposure is large enough that even a partial collateral impairment has systemic reach. BIS data
indicate non-U.S. banks' on-balance-sheet dollar debt totaled approximately $26 trillion as of mid-2022, with an additional
$39 trillion in off-balance-sheet dollar obligations embedded in FX swaps and forwards — payment obligations that do not
appear on balance sheets and that, in combination, exceed the entire on-balance-sheet stock of dollar Treasury bills, repo,
and commercial paper (Bank for International Settlements, 2022). This "missing dollar debt" functions as a continuous
rollover obligation, collateralized in large part by the same pool of high-quality dollar assets whose supply and price are
affected by U.S. fiscal conditions. A widening of U.S. term premia that simultaneously erodes the value of foreign-held
Treasury collateral and raises the cost of fresh dollar borrowing compounds, rather than offsets, the stress on this rollover
stock.
The transmission channel into FX funding markets operates through the cross-currency basis — the premium non-U.S.
institutions pay, relative to covered interest parity, to borrow dollars via the FX swap market rather than directly. BIS
research established that this basis, which should theoretically be zero under no-arbitrage conditions, persistently departs
from zero in the direction reflecting dollar scarcity and widens further during episodes of dollar funding stress (Borio,
McCauley, McGuire, and Sushko, 2016). A decline in foreign demand for dollar assets — whether from active reserve
diversification, as documented above, or from a fiscal-risk-driven repricing of the kind modeled in Section 5.4 — reduces
the supply of dollars available through the channels that would otherwise keep the basis near parity, pushing non-U.S.
institutions toward the FX swap market and widening the basis they pay. In practice, this raises the effective cost of dollar
funding for European and Asian banks regardless of the policy rate set by their home central bank — the mechanism that
produced acute dollar funding stress in the 2008 crisis and again in March 2020 (Cecchetti and Schoenholtz, 2020).
The policy backstop for this channel is the Federal Reserve's standing U.S. dollar liquidity swap line network with the Bank
of Canada, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the European Central Bank, and the Swiss National Bank — converted
from temporary to standing arrangements in October 2013 and expanded to nine additional central banks on a temporary
basis during the March 2020 dollar funding squeeze (Federal Reserve Board, 2020; Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond,
2024). These swap lines function as a release valve for the mechanism described above: by giving foreign central banks
direct access to dollars without forcing private institutions through the FX swap market, they cap the price spike that would
otherwise result and reduce the incentive for foreign holders to liquidate dollar assets, including Treasuries, into a stressed
market (Brookings Institution, 2025). Their reliability has itself recently become a subject of discussion among foreign
officials, following reporting that some foreign central bankers have begun privately asking whether a future Federal
Reserve leadership would extend dollar liquidity as readily as it did during the 2008 and 2020 episodes (Brookings
Institution, 2025). Whatever the resolution of that question, its emergence is itself a supervisory data point: a channel
previously treated as a structural certainty is now, at the margin, a variable — and a fiscal trajectory that erodes confidence

The net effect of this section's argument is to recast reserve-currency erosion from a borrowing-cost problem into a
global-collateral and global-funding problem. The financing-cost benefit estimated by McKinsey Global Institute (2009)
and CEPR/VoxEU (2024) measures what the United States gains from supplying the world's primary safe asset. The
contribution here is to make explicit what the rest of the world stands to lose — and to transmit back into U.S. funding
markets — if that asset's quality is impaired by the trajectory described in Section 2, a linkage Section 5.2 develops further
through the foreign banking organizations that intermediate much of this exposure inside the United States, and that Section
5.4 incorporates into the Treasury-market stress scenario directly.
The privilege partially finances the debt while also generating pressures that may weigh on the productive base from which
that debt must ultimately be serviced — and, as shown above, generating a collateral and funding dependency running
through the global financial system that a purely domestic borrowing-cost framing does not capture. From a supervisory
standpoint, an advantage whose long-run magnitude cannot be assumed constant, whose erosion has a stated transmission
path into the term premium, and whose downstream effects extend to the balance sheets of foreign central banks, foreign
commercial banks, and the foreign banking organizations operating inside the United States, warrants more cautious
treatment in fiscal sustainability analysis than a static reading of current financing conditions would suggest.
3.2 Security Expenditure Asymmetry
The United States maintains approximately 750 military installations across 80 countries. SIPRI data indicate that U.S.
military spending reached $997 billion in 2024 — 37 percent of total world military expenditure and 66 percent of all
NATO spending combined, using SIPRI's methodology that includes military-related spending across federal agencies
beyond the Department of Defense (SIPRI, 2025). European NATO members would have required an additional $663
billion to reach a 5 percent of GDP alliance standard (SIPRI, 2025).

3.3 Deindustrialization and the Financialization Transition
Manufacturing's share of U.S. GDP fell from approximately 27 percent in 1950 to around 11 percent today, with the finance,
insurance, and real estate sector surpassing manufacturing as a share of domestic production as early as 1991 (Bureau of
Economic Analysis, NIPA Table 1.3.5). The most directly traceable fiscal implication of this structural shift operates
through the differential tax-revenue elasticity of different forms of economic activity — a relationship the mainstream
empirical literature quantifies with reasonable consistency, and one that can be stated as a bounded coefficient rather than
only as a directional claim.
Define the tax-revenue elasticity of a sector as ε_sector = (%∆Tax Revenue) / (%∆Sector Value Added). Moretti
(2010) estimates a tradable-sector local employment multiplier of 1.6 to 2.0 — each direct job in the tradable sector supports
one to two additional positions in the local service economy through income and spending linkages, a relationship that does
not hold at the same magnitude for financial-services employment, which is more geographically concentrated and generates
narrower local consumption spillovers (Moretti, 2010). Applying the combined OASDI and HI payroll tax rate of 15.3
percent to this multiplier yields a bounded payroll-tax-base capture range:

Share of Value Added × 0.153
For tradable manufacturing, where compensation is predominantly ordinary wages, this implies approximately 2.6× to 3.0×

4. A Productive-Capacity Framework
Traditional discussions of debt sustainability often stop at fiscal consolidation: lower spending, higher taxes, or some
combination. Those measures matter, but they are incomplete if the underlying economy is losing productive momentum.
Bernanke wrote that public policy should support workforce skills, private capital formation, research and development, and
public infrastructure; Yellen emphasized that delay makes adjustment more painful and less controllable (Bernanke, 2011;
Yellen, 2010). The debt arithmetic in Section 2 formalizes the same point: any policy that raises trend growth narrows r–g
and reduces the primary surplus required for stabilization.
Productive capacity, as used here, denotes the set of conditions that sustain broad-based output growth over time:
high-quality physical infrastructure, reliable energy systems, a capable and accessible labor force, frontier technology, and
institutions that support long-term investment. That is a narrower and more defensible concept than the claim that the United
States can simply 'grow out' of its debt — a claim this paper does not make. The argument is that stronger productive

capacity improves the probability that debt dynamics remain manageable without requiring abrupt fiscal adjustment, and
that the structure of the economy materially affects the burden of public liabilities, as the Geithner (2005) speech on
productivity and fiscal sustainability observed directly.
A more specific concept is essential to the argument: productive indispensability. Standard endogenous growth theory
(Romer, 1990) establishes that knowledge-intensive sectors generate positive externalities — non-rival ideas that spill over
to other producers. Productive indispensability is a more sector-specific analytical claim: it refers to sectors whose outputs
function as high-value, difficult-to-substitute inputs to global production systems in ways that are associated with switching
costs that may generate sustained sourcing inertia. The distinction matters for fiscal analysis. An industry generating
knowledge spillovers raises g broadly. An industry generating structural dependencies may raise g while also influencing
sovereign financing conditions through channels — including reserve currency demand and the broader conditions under
which international economic arrangements are sustained — that do not appear in standard productivity accounting and are
difficult to quantify precisely.
The empirical test for productive indispensability is therefore not simply high R&D intensity or TFP contribution, but
whether the sector's absence would require other economies to undertake costly restructuring of their production systems
around alternatives — a test that advanced semiconductor lithography, foundational AI infrastructure, and GPS-dependent
logistics networks plausibly appear more likely to meet than standardized financial intermediation services. As the
quantitative matrix below shows, however, "foundational AI infrastructure" is not analytically uniform: the
chip-design-and-fabrication layer underlying it exhibits substantially greater concentration than the cloud-services layer
through which most economic activity accesses compute, and the two should not be scored as a single category.
A second qualification precedes the matrix itself and bears directly on the fiscal mechanisms claimed below. The geographic
location of productive indispensability and the location of its fiscal benefit are not the same variable. A sector can be
globally indispensable — meaning its absence would force costly restructuring of world production systems — without that
indispensability being domestically sited, in which case the payroll- and corporate-tax-base benefits described later in this
section do not accrue to the United States in proportion to the sector's global importance. This distinction is made explicit in
the scoring methodology below and should be read as a qualification on, not a rejection of, the productive-indispensability
argument: it sharpens which sectors actually deliver the fiscal mechanism this paper describes, as opposed to which sectors
are merely globally critical.
A third qualification is geopolitical rather than economic in character, and it precedes the matrix for the same reason. The
empirical test above implicitly assumes that a sector's current configuration is stable — that access to it is a function of
engineering and capital constraints rather than of contested territorial control or discretionary foreign export licensing. That
assumption fails for the two highest-scoring sectors in the matrix below. Advanced-node semiconductor fabrication is
concentrated in Taiwan, separated from mainland China by a strait through which approximately 20 percent of global
maritime trade and 44 percent of the world's container fleet transit in ordinary conditions, and across which industry risk
analysts citing Bloomberg Economics estimates put the global GDP at risk from a sustained military conflict or blockade at
on the order of $10 trillion, or roughly 10 percent of world GDP (Bloomberg Economics, as reported in semiconductor
supply-chain risk analysis, 2026). EUV lithography is concentrated in a single Dutch firm whose ability to sell, service, and
maintain its most advanced tools is currently subject to an active and unresolved dispute between the U.S. and Dutch
governments over the scope of export controls to China — a dispute the Dutch trade minister was still actively lobbying
Washington to narrow as of late June 2026 (Bloomberg, 2026; NL Times, 2026). Productive indispensability, in other
words, cannot be reduced to a question of what can be manufactured domestically. It is conditional on the continued stability
of a small number of fragile bilateral arrangements — between Washington and Taipei, and between Washington and The
Hague — that lie outside the control of U.S. fiscal or industrial policy in the near term. The scoring framework below makes
this conditionality explicit rather than leaving it as a background assumption.

Five criteria are each converted to a 0–10 score from a defined metric. Four combine into the weighted composite PI Index
used in earlier drafts of this framework; the fifth is applied separately, as a discount, for the reason explained below.
Global Supply Concentration (GSC) is measured on two sub-dimensions. Market structure is captured by the
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, HHI = Σ(s_i)², where s_i is each firm's global market share in percentage points. Standard
DOJ/FTC Horizontal Merger Guidelines bands are applied: HHI below 1,500 is unconcentrated (score 1–3); 1,500–2,500 is
moderately concentrated (score 4–6); above 2,500 is highly concentrated (score 7–10, scaling toward 10 as HHI approaches
its theoretical maximum of 10,000 under monopoly). Separately, U.S./allied production share — the percentage of global
output produced domestically or by treaty allies — is tracked because, as shown below, market concentration and U.S. fiscal
capture frequently diverge.
Switching Cost Horizon (SCH) combines the capital expenditure required to build an alternative supply chain at equivalent
capability, normalized against the incumbent's annual sector revenue, with the time required to reach yield or quality parity
(not merely first output). Low CapEx-and-time combinations score 1–3; moderate combinations score 4–6; CapEx
exceeding twice incumbent annual revenue combined with a replication horizon exceeding seven years scores 7–10.
Downstream Breadth (DB) is measured via BEA Input-Output Total Requirements Tables: the count of three-digit NAICS
sectors for which the candidate sector constitutes a non-trivial share (above 2 percent) of intermediate input costs, combined
with the sector's total-output multiplier. A multiplier above 2.5 or dependence by more than fifteen downstream sectors
scores 7–10.
R&D Intensity (RDI) is R&D expenditure as a share of sector revenue, benchmarked against the U.S. national
R&D-to-GDP ratio of 3.43 percent (NSF/NCSES, 2025) and the federally funded R&D share of GDP, which stood at 0.63
percent in 2024 — its lowest sustained level since the 1950s (NSF/NCSES, 2026). Intensity exceeding twice the national
baseline scores 7–10.
The composite index weights concentration and switching cost most heavily, consistent with the empirical test stated above:
PI Index = 0.30 × GSC + 0.30 × SCH + 0.25 × DB + 0.15 × RDI
Geopolitical Fragility Discount (GFD) captures a dimension the four criteria above do not: whether the sector's
indispensability is conditional on uninterrupted access to a single contested transit corridor, a single non-allied or
contested-sovereignty jurisdiction, or a single foreign government's export-licensing discretion. It is scored 0–10, where 0–3
reflects geographically diversified production with no single chokepoint or licensing dependency; 4–7 reflects concentration
in a single stable allied jurisdiction whose export posture is nonetheless subject to active bilateral negotiation; and 8–10
reflects concentration in a jurisdiction exposed to an active territorial dispute, a contested strait, or comparable military risk.
GFD does not enter the PI Index as a fifth additive term, because it measures a different property — fragility of access rather
than depth of indispensability. It is instead applied as a multiplicative discount:
PI_adjusted = PI Index × (1 − GFD/20)
so that a sector scoring at the ceiling of structural indispensability but also at the ceiling of geopolitical fragility is
discounted by up to 50 percent, while a sector with no meaningful chokepoint exposure retains its full PI Index score.

KEY SUPERVISORY FINDING
Productive indispensability cannot be manufactured at home. The risk-adjusted ranking in Table 8 shows that the sector with
the strongest raw case for domestic reshoring — advanced-node fabrication — is also the sector whose effective
indispensability is most undermined by a risk the United States cannot resolve through domestic capital allocation alone.
Sustaining productive indispensability in this category requires managing the trade, diplomatic, and security alliances — with
Taiwan specifically, and with the Netherlands on a different but related axis — on which access to the sector's current
configuration depends. A supervisory framework that prices semiconductor indispensability without pricing the geopolitical
fragility of its supply chain will systematically overstate the reliability of the fiscal mechanism this section otherwise
describes.
4.3 Fiscal Mechanisms and Historical Precedent
The current AI investment cycle exhibits several characteristics historically associated with platform-level technology
transitions: the infrastructure buildout for large-scale compute — data centers, power systems, specialized chip fabrication,

immediate application layer. Whether the breadth of that diffusion approaches earlier general-purpose technology waves
remains an open empirical question. The geographic dimension is, however, more directly grounded in available fiscal

and engineering employment generate taxable payroll and corporate revenue at each stage of the value chain in ways that
supply chains heavily concentrated offshore do not, at least not to the same degree — a distinction relevant to the revenue

buildout than to the chip-fabrication layer underlying it — itself the layer carrying the highest unadjusted indispensability
score and the highest geopolitical discount.
The structural incentive mismatch between private capital markets and platform-level innovation is worth stating precisely,
because it directly bears on the fiscal case for public research investment. Venture capital and private equity operate under
return horizons and portfolio-construction disciplines that tend to under-incentivize the category of research most relevant to
productive indispensability. The median venture fund targets exit within five to eight years; the payoff horizon for
foundational technological platforms — the kind that create new industries rather than improve existing products —
typically runs fifteen to twenty-five years from initial research to broad economic diffusion. Private capital funds the later
commercialization stages once platforms are sufficiently de-risked. It rarely funds the earlier, longer, and riskier research
phases that determine whether a platform-level advance becomes available at all. GPS had no private-sector funder before it
was operational; the foundational research underlying mRNA vaccines was sustained by NIH grants for decades before any
commercial application was apparent. The implication for fiscal policy is not that government should replace private capital
in commercialization — it should not — but that the public R&D budget is not functionally equivalent to private R&D
spending, and declining it toward 0.63 percent of GDP from its Cold War peak of 1.86 percent leaves a structural gap in the
innovation pipeline that private markets are unlikely to close on their own (NSF/NCSES, 2026).
Three fiscal mechanisms follow from productive indispensability specifically, qualified now by both the
geographic-versus-fiscal-capture distinction and the geopolitical-fragility discount above. First, manufacturing and
technology ecosystems tend to generate broader tax revenues than financial intermediation, per the elasticity coefficients in
Table 6: a dollar of manufacturing value added supports substantially more employment, and therefore more payroll,
income, and sales tax revenue, than a dollar of financial activity, improving the revenue term of the fiscal identity, all else
equal — provided that activity is both domestically sited and not contingent on an unresolved geopolitical chokepoint.
Second, there is historical evidence that productive leadership has been associated with a broader set of instruments for
sustaining international institutional commitments — an association that, while difficult to isolate in a formal accounting
framework, may bear on the non-market components of sovereign financing conditions over longer horizons. Third,
productive leadership appears to be associated with relatively stable international demand for a nation's bonds and currency,
a pattern consistent with Eichengreen's historical analysis. The dollar's reserve status reflects both the institutional
architecture established at Bretton Woods and the productivity of the American economy that made dollar-denominated
assets attractive to hold. Eichengreen's analysis suggests that financial privilege and productive leadership have tended to
develop together rather than the former systematically preceding the latter (Eichengreen, 2011) — a pattern consistent with
the complementarity argument of this paper.
The historical record supports the basic intuition, though it should be read with care given the institutional and monetary
differences across cases. Britain's reduction of public debt from approximately 260 percent of GDP in 1815 to roughly 30
percent by 1900 resulted primarily from industrialization rather than from primary-surplus discipline (Office for Budget
Responsibility, 2023). The postwar American experience illustrates the mechanism more directly: debt at 106 percent of
GDP in 1945 was reduced substantially through sustained economic expansion associated with broad-based productivity
gains and the expansion of American industrial and technological capacity, not through austerity alone. Historical episodes
in which productive capacity eroded ahead of sovereign financing conditions — including the Dutch Republic in the
eighteenth century — are suggestive, though the monetary structures and institutional contexts differ enough from a modern

5. Financial Stability and Supervisory Implications
For supervisory purposes, the analytically relevant question is transmission: how does an elevated sovereign debt trajectory
translate into risks for financial institutions? High sovereign debt does not become a banking crisis automatically. The
pathway runs through specific channels that supervisors can monitor, stress-test, and incorporate into institution-level risk
assessment. Four channels warrant particular attention; each, as developed below, has both a domestic and a cross-border
dimension.
5.1 Duration and Valuation Risk in Securities Portfolios
Large banking organizations and many non-bank intermediaries hold significant quantities of Treasury and agency
securities. These holdings serve important liquidity and collateral functions but create duration exposure. In a higher-yield
environment — a plausible scenario if term premia reprice in response to fiscal deterioration or a loss of fiscal credibility —
unrealized losses accumulate even when credit quality remains strong. The Federal Reserve's April 2025 Financial Stability
Report states explicitly that debt-servicing costs for governments and corporations would rise if interest rates increased and
that those dynamics could produce fair-value losses on fixed-rate securities among financial intermediaries, reducing credit
supply (Federal Reserve Board, 2025). The 2022–2023 experience with regional banking stress under rapid rate increases
illustrates that this transmission channel is real, operationally significant, and capable of moving more quickly than
supervisory cycle times. A sovereign-risk-driven repricing would differ in origin but not in mechanism; Section 5.4
calibrates this channel against that episode directly and quantifies the resulting capital impact. Section 3.1 shows that the
same duration mechanics apply, in parallel, to the Treasury holdings of foreign central banks and foreign commercial banks,
meaning the domestic valuation channel modeled here has a foreign-balance-sheet mirror that this paper treats as part of the
same transmission event rather than a separate one.
5.2 Wholesale Funding Pressure
Deteriorating fiscal confidence, even short of a market event, can increase wholesale funding costs for institutions with
significant reliance on short-term liabilities. This is particularly relevant for non-bank financial intermediaries — money
market funds, repo-market participants, and others whose structural position in Treasury markets has grown materially over
the past decade. The BIS has framed this in directly relevant terms: high government debt combined with the growing
presence of non-bank financial institutions in sovereign bond markets creates policy challenges that cut across fiscal,
monetary, and prudential domains (Bank for International Settlements, 2025).
This domestic framing, however, omits the institutions most structurally exposed to exactly this channel: the U.S. branches
and agencies of foreign banking organizations (FBOs). Most FBO branches lack access to FDIC-insured deposits and
consequently depend on wholesale funding — large time deposits, repo, commercial paper, and interbank borrowing — for
nearly all of their dollar needs (Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia, 2019). This structural feature makes FBOs, in the
language of New York Fed research, "marginal price setters" of the price of dollar liquidity in wholesale funding markets,
and positions them as the principal bridge between onshore U.S. dollar funding markets and the offshore dollar markets in
Europe and Asia described in Section 3.1 (Du, 2023). Historically, FBO branches have operated a "funding branch" model
in which the U.S. branch borrows large quantities of wholesale dollars and upstreams them to the parent organization;
branches operating on this model rose from holding 40 percent of FBO branch assets in the mid-1990s to 75 percent by
2009, and foreign banks collectively moved from net recipients of parent funding in 1999 to net providers of more than $600


VERS3DYNAMICS | MAY 2026 Fiscal Sustainability & the Structural Headwinds to Debt Stabilization
Vers3Dynamics Page 17 FOR PUBLIC RELEASE
billion to non-U.S. affiliates by 2008 (Tarullo, 2014). In ordinary times, the U.S. dollar wholesale funding market is not
merely a domestic financing venue for FBOs; it is a source of dollars that FBOs distribute to their global organizations.
That structure reverses, predictably, during stress, and the reversal is the transmission mechanism most relevant to the
fiscal-risk scenario this paper models. During the 2011 European sovereign debt crisis, U.S. money market funds —
proximate, risk-averse investors with no appetite for European sovereign exposure even by association — sharply reduced
their funding of European banks' U.S. branches. European parent banks responded by initiating internal liquidity transfers to
their U.S. branches to offset the shortfall, and the U.S. branches of euro-area banks became net borrowers vis-à-vis their
own head offices in the second half of 2011 for the first time in several years (Federal Reserve Board, International Finance
Discussion Papers No. 1059, 2012). The parents absorbed the cost: their own dollar funding costs rose at the same time,
since they had to source the dollars to inject — whether through their own wholesale operations or, per Section 3.1, through
the FX swap market at a widened cross-currency basis — preventing the liquidity shortfall at the U.S. branch from being
offset completely.
The fiscal-risk scenario in this paper runs the same mechanism with the shock originating on the U.S. side rather than the
European side. A sharp repricing of Treasury-market term premium — the 200 basis point shock modeled in Section 5.4 —
raises wholesale dollar funding costs in the United States directly, through the Tier 1 and Tier 2 collateral-spread widening
quantified in Table 12. FBO branches, as structural net borrowers of overnight wholesale dollars with no FDIC-insured
deposit base to fall back on, are first in line to absorb that repricing. As in 2011, the likely institutional response is for the
parent organization — typically headquartered in London, Frankfurt, Tokyo, or another major financial center — to
mobilize home-currency liquidity and swap it into dollars to backstop the New York or Washington branch, rather than
allow the branch to curtail its U.S. dollar lending and risk reputational and regulatory consequences in its most important
foreign market. That mobilization draws on the parent's home-market liquidity and balance sheet capacity and is transacted,
in large part, through the same FX swap market whose basis is already widening under the conditions described in Section
3.1 — meaning the domestic and cross-border channels reinforce rather than operate independently of each other. A U.S.
Treasury-market event that might otherwise be analyzed as a contained domestic balance-sheet problem is, through this
channel, transmitted directly into the funding markets and balance sheets of foreign parent banks, at the precise moment
those parent banks are least able to absorb additional stress cheaply.
The scale of this exposure historically has been large enough to matter at the system level. As of December 2013 — a
snapshot from the post-crisis period when FBO balance sheets were unusually cash-heavy following Dodd-Frank
implementation — foreign bank branches held approximately 43 percent of total reserves at the Federal Reserve, despite
FBO total U.S. assets being a fraction of domestic banks' (Bank for International Settlements, 2014). That figure is dated
and should not be read as a current estimate, but it illustrates the order of magnitude of FBO presence inside the U.S.
wholesale funding and reserves system; the FFIEC 002 reports that track current branch-level assets, liabilities, and funding

5.3 Policy-Space Compression
The most diffuse but potentially most consequential supervisory implication is the progressive compression of policy space
available in future downturns. When fiscal consolidation is deferred until a stress event, the adjustment tends to be abrupt —
with larger multiplier effects on output than a gradual adjustment would produce. Blanchard and Leigh found that fiscal
multipliers in the post-2008 period ran approximately 1.5, meaning that for every dollar cut in a high-stress environment,
economic output fell by roughly $1.50 (Blanchard and Leigh, 2013). Bernanke noted this dynamic directly: adjustments
occurring in an orderly and gradual way are considerably better than those occurring abruptly in response to a market crisis
(Bernanke, 2011). A financial system operating against a backdrop of compressed fiscal space is structurally more fragile
than one with room for countercyclical response — the Federal Reserve's own monetary policy transmission is affected by
the degree to which fiscal policy can amplify or dampen its actions.
5.4 Treasury-Market Functioning: A Quantitative Stress-Testing Framework
The Federal Reserve's 2026 stress-test scenarios assume severe global recession and large asset-price shocks (Federal
Reserve Board, 2026). Those scenarios are not sovereign-debt stress tests, but they provide a useful benchmark for the speed
at which market conditions can deteriorate. A supervisory scenario focused on sovereign risk would not require assuming
default; it would only need to examine whether a sharper repricing of fiscal risk, a more abrupt yield increase, or a loss of
market confidence could worsen bank capital and liquidity outcomes under otherwise comparable macro conditions. This
subsection operationalizes that scenario, calibrated against the 2022–2023 regional banking episode and expressed as a
duration-based capital-impact model, then extends the aggregation to the global channels developed in Sections 3.1 and 5.2.

KEY SUPERVISORY FINDING
The duration/CET1 channel and the wholesale-funding channel operate on different clocks, and the cross-border extension of
each operates on a third. The CET1 channel is a quarterly-disclosure phenomenon that, per the worked example in Table 11,
erodes capital ratios meaningfully but does not by itself produce insolvency for an adequately capitalized institution even
under a 200 bps shock. The domestic funding channel is an intraday-to-multiday phenomenon that can force the asset sales
converting unrealized losses into realized ones — this was the actual proximate mechanism in the SVB failure. The
cross-border channel adds a fourth clock again: it is the speed at which a foreign parent bank can mobilize home-market
liquidity and access the FX swap market, which the 2011 precedent suggests is fast enough to transmit a funding shock
internationally within the same stress episode, not as a delayed second-order effect. Supervisory analysis that treats the
domestic and cross-border channels as sequential rather than concurrent will misjudge how quickly a U.S. fiscal-stress event
becomes a global one.

6. Policy Options
The relevant policy conclusion is that the United States requires a coherent mixed strategy in which fiscal consolidation and
productivity-enhancing investment are treated as complements rather than alternatives. Bernanke's formulation remains
analytically useful: a more productive economy eases the tradeoffs, but it does not eliminate the need for fiscal adjustment
(Bernanke, 2011). Each lever carries its own costs and timing profile; the question is not which lever is always superior, but
whether the policy mix is coherent over time and consistent with the Federal Reserve's supervisory mandate — domestically
and, per Section 5, internationally.
6.1 Entitlement and Health-Care Adjustment
Mandatory spending on Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid constitutes roughly 60 percent of the federal budget and
grows automatically with demographics and medical cost inflation, without annual appropriations votes. This is the primary
structural driver of the long-run debt path. Discretionary non-defense spending — the category most accessible in annual
budget negotiations — represents less than 15 percent of total federal outlays. Addressing the long-run debt trajectory
without engaging mandatory spending is, as a matter of arithmetic, not possible at the required scale. Entitlement reform is
necessary; it is also the category where near-term adjustment has the largest aggregate effect on long-run fiscal
sustainability.
6.2 Revenue Measures
The United States collects significantly less revenue as a share of GDP than most comparable advanced economies. As Saez
and Zucman document, the distributional structure of American taxation has shifted substantially over the past four decades
(Saez and Zucman, 2019). Broad-based revenue measures, designed to minimize growth distortions, can reduce annual
deficits directly and improve the primary balance term of the debt identity. The net impact on debt dynamics depends
critically on the extent to which the revenue structure supports or suppresses productive investment — a revenue system that
concentrates the burden on capital formation could offset some of the debt-stabilization gains by further suppressing the
denominator. The Section 3.1 balance-of-payments decomposition is relevant here as well: revenue measures that affect the
attractiveness of dollar-denominated assets to foreign holders interact with the financial-account financing channel, the
global collateral channel, and the cross-currency basis that together help determine the term premium embedded in r.
6.3 Productivity-Enhancing Public Investment
Federal R&D spending has declined from approximately 1.86 percent of GDP in 1964 — during the Cold War space and
defense investment programs — to 0.63 percent in 2024, its lowest sustained level since before that peak (NSF/NCSES,
2026). Private R&D has partially filled the gap, but private capital concentrates heavily on near-term commercialization.
Platform-level advances that create new industries rather than improving existing ones — the category most relevant to
productive indispensability — typically require the patient, long-horizon research that private capital structures do not
efficiently incentivize. The internet did not originate from a venture capital portfolio. Neither did GPS nor the Human
Genome Project. The federal government's role is not to select commercial winners; it is to fund the research frontier from
which commercially valuable output emerges. The energy grid, immigration pathways for STEM talent, and
university-industry research pipelines function as productive infrastructure rather than consumption spending. Each has the
potential to strengthen the denominator of the debt ratio over medium-term horizons through channels — productivity
diffusion, labor-force quality, and research commercialization — that operate with longer lags than conventional fiscal
policy but tend to be more durable when sustained across investment cycles. The scenario analysis in Section 2 provides a
rough quantitative anchor for the fiscal arithmetic: each 50 basis point improvement in trend growth reduces the primary
surplus required for debt stabilization by approximately 0.5 percentage points of GDP — roughly $145 billion annually at
current nominal GDP. Well-targeted productive-capacity investment that contributes even a fraction of that improvement
over a decade would generate fiscal returns that offset a meaningful portion of its near-term cost, provided the composition

The geopolitical-fragility discount developed in Section 4 carries a further implication for this composition choice.
Domestic capital allocation toward advanced semiconductor fabrication addresses the revenue-capture gap identified in

6.4 Debt Management Strategy
Maturity extension on outstanding debt can reduce rollover risk and smooth financing conditions independently of the
primary balance. This lever is available to the Treasury as a policy tool and directly relevant to supervisory analysis:
institutions holding short-duration Treasuries are less exposed to repricing risk than those holding long duration, per the
duration mechanics quantified in Section 5.4 — a relevance that extends to the foreign central banks and commercial banks
discussed in Section 3.1, whose own Treasury portfolio duration affects how much of the global collateral channel a given
term-premium shock activates. A more actively managed maturity profile reduces the interest-rate shock transmission
channel discussed in Section 5.1 and buys time for the structural adjustments in Sections 6.1 through 6.3 to take effect. It
does not reduce the total stock of debt; it reduces the financial-stability risk of that stock under adverse rate scenarios,
domestically and abroad.
The political economy of this mixed strategy deserves acknowledgment. Fiscal consolidation and productive investment can
appear to pull in opposite directions over short budget horizons: consolidation requires reducing near-term spending, while
productive investment requires sustaining or expanding certain categories of it. The tension is real but manageable if both
tracks are designed with explicit attention to timing and composition. Consolidation concentrated in mandatory program
reform and revenue adequacy preserves space for productive investment; productive investment targeted at sectors with
measurable spillover characteristics minimizes the near-term fiscal cost of the growth strategy. Institutional continuity —
specifically, the insulation of long-horizon research programs from annual appropriations volatility — is itself a form of
policy design that improves the expected return on productive-capacity investment.

7. Limitations
No new structural model is estimated, and no specific productivity initiative is claimed to produce a guaranteed macro
payoff. The r–g differential is endogenous, as noted above, and the productive-capacity framework should be read as a
structure for thinking about long-run fiscal resilience rather than a substitute for primary-balance arithmetic.
There is also a supervisory boundary to respect. The Federal Reserve does not set fiscal policy and should not be portrayed
as responsible for closing the budget gap. Its role is to evaluate how fiscal developments affect financial institutions,
markets, and the transmission of monetary policy. The analysis here stays within that mandate: it identifies fiscal conditions
that raise supervisory risk, maps the channels through which that risk propagates domestically and across borders, and
points to the policy complements — including productive investment and alliance management — that would improve those
conditions. It does not recommend a specific fiscal package, and it does not take a position on the substance of any specific
bilateral trade or export-control negotiation; it treats the existence and fragility of those negotiations as a supervisory input,
not a policy recommendation.
The historical comparisons in Section 4 deserve explicit qualification. Britain's nineteenth-century debt reduction and the
Dutch Republic's sovereign default in 1810 involved monetary structures, institutional arrangements, and geopolitical
contexts materially different from those of a modern fiat reserve currency issuer. They are cited because they are consistent
with the basic debt-arithmetic proposition that trend growth is a first-order determinant of debt trajectory; they are not
presented as structurally parallel cases. Applying the specific debt ratios or timelines from those episodes to the United
States would be inappropriate.
The counterarguments to the productive-capacity thesis also deserve acknowledgment. Gordon's work on the innovation
slowdown presents serious empirical evidence that transformative productivity gains may be behind us and that current
frontier technologies may be less economically powerful than those of earlier industrial eras (Gordon, 2016). Summers's
secular stagnation thesis makes a related structural argument about persistent demand shortfalls and low equilibrium interest
rates (Summers, 2014). If either analysis is substantially correct, the growth-driven component of debt stabilization is less
available than the framework assumes, and the weight of adjustment falls more heavily on primary balances. That is a real
limitation and an argument for urgency on both tracks rather than sequencing one behind the other.
A further limitation involves the endogeneity of productive investment timing. Sustained public investment in R&D,
infrastructure, and education raises near-term spending, which adds to debt before the productivity payoff materializes. The
long payoff horizon means that the fiscal case for productive investment is sensitive to discount rates: at sufficiently high
discount rates, the net present value of growth returns may not justify the near-term addition to debt. The framework does
not resolve this uncertainty; it argues that the expected return is positive and that the composition of investment matters —
frontier research with measurable spillover characteristics carries higher expected fiscal returns than undifferentiated public
spending, and, per Section 4.2, this composition effect is strongest for domestically sited activity specifically. That claim
should be held with appropriate epistemic humility.
Blanchard's (2019) finding that r < g has historically been the norm in advanced economies provides a partial offset to the
Gordon–Summers pessimism: if trend growth can be sustained, fiscal dynamics may be more benign than CBO's baseline
interest assumptions imply. But that result strengthens rather than weakens the productive-capacity argument, since
maintaining g above r requires exactly the structural conditions this paper describes.

8. Conclusion
The United States is not facing an immediate debt crisis, but it is on a fiscal trajectory that compounds the financial-stability
risks that supervisors monitor — and, this revision has argued, that other countries' financial systems monitor as well,
whether or not their own supervisors frame it in those terms. CBO's baseline projects a decade of rising debt ratios and
increasing net interest costs, driven by mandatory spending growth and a revenue structure that has not kept pace with
federal obligations. Three structural headwinds — reserve currency costs, security expenditure asymmetries, and
deindustrialization — compound that trajectory in ways that standard fiscal analysis does not always capture, and that are
not fully addressable by conventional consolidation tools alone. This revision has shown that the deindustrialization channel
can be expressed as a bounded tax-elasticity coefficient rather than only a directional claim, that the reserve-currency
channel maps formally onto the financial-account side of the balance-of-payments identity and onward into a global
collateral and cross-currency-basis mechanism, and that the productive-indispensability sectors most often invoked in this
debate divide sharply along two axes that the raw engineering case for reshoring does not capture: whether the activity is
domestically sited, and whether its indispensability is geopolitically conditional rather than structural.
The central supervisory lesson is that sovereign debt dynamics are not solely a matter for budget committees, and not solely
a matter for U.S. supervisors. They shape Treasury-market functioning, term premia, bank valuation exposure, funding
conditions, and the policy space available when the next downturn arrives — and, through the foreign banking organizations
and global collateral channels developed in Sections 3.1 and 5.2, they shape the same variables for financial systems in
Europe and Asia that depend on the dollar and on Treasury securities to fund themselves. The Federal Reserve's own
Financial Stability Report has explicitly identified the domestic version of this risk channel; this paper's contribution is to
show that the channel does not stop at the U.S. border. Section 5.4 has shown that the domestic channel can be calibrated
quantitatively against the 2022–2023 regional banking episode, that its duration-driven and funding-driven components
operate on different clocks, and that both have a cross-border mirror — in foreign central bank and commercial bank

Durable debt stabilization therefore functions as more than a domestic fiscal exercise. It requires credible primary-balance
adjustment, policies that sustain trend growth strong enough to keep r–g from widening further, and — as Section 4's
risk-adjusted matrix makes explicit — sustained management of the alliance relationships on which the geopolitically
concentrated portion of U.S. productive indispensability depends. The first reduces the numerator of the debt ratio; the

conditions outside U.S. control. Fiscal consolidation without productive capacity risks contracting the economy faster than
the debt. Productive investment without fiscal discipline risks compounding near-term obligations while waiting for

three, conducted without attention to the global collateral and funding channels this revision has detailed, risk treating as a
domestic balance-sheet problem what is, in fact, the foundational anchor of global dollar liquidity and, through it, of global
financial stability. Managed as complements, and grounded in institutional continuity across political cycles, fiscal
discipline, productive investment, and alliance management together represent the most defensible path to maintaining
monetary and financial stability over the long run — for the United States, and for the financial systems that depend on it.
